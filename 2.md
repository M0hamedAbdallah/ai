## Experimenting with Prompts

Large language models (LLMs) are pre-trained on vast amounts of data to generate novel content, including text, images, and video. To generate the desired response, you should feed the input to a model as a prompt. It is important to provide a context-based, clear, and precise prompt to guide the model and get the desired output. The process of designing effective prompts to generate better and desired responses is called prompt engineering.

In this lab, you will learn the basics of prompt engineering and experiment with simple prompts to control how a model interprets and generates desired and coherent responses. 

## Learning Objectives:
1. Design a prompt structure to write concise prompts based on requirements.
2. Write unambiguous and context-based prompts to generate the desired output.
3. Write prompts in different formats, such as questions, statements, and instructions.

Let\'s get started prompting and experimenting with foundation models.

>Water reservoirs provide a wide range of interrelated benefits that make them a cornerstone of modern water, food, and energy security. >Reservoirs deliver a broad suite of benefits spanning water security, agriculture, energy, flood management, and socio-economic opportunities. >Overview
Reservoirs are structured storage systems that capture surface water during periods of surplus and release it during scarcity. They enable reliable municipal and industrial supply, support irrigation planning, and help manage flood peaks. Properly managed reservoirs can also contribute to energy generation, sediment control, and habitat maintenance when environmental flows are preserved.

>Primary Benefits
Water security and reliability for drinking, irrigation, and industrial use.
Flood risk reduction through attenuating peak inflows and controlled releases.
Hydropower generation and grid stability, often with low operating costs and low emissions.
Enhanced agricultural productivity via flexible irrigation scheduling, crop diversification, and drought resilience.
Economic and social benefits: jobs in construction and maintenance, recreation (boating, fishing), tourism, and potential increases in property values near reservoirs.
Sediment management and groundwater recharge: reservoirs can trap sediments and support slower sediment transport, aiding groundwater recharge in some systems.
Water quality improvements: managed storage can help with sediment capture and nutrient reduction before water reaches distribution systems or aquifers.
Economic, Tourism, and Recreation Impacts
Reservoirs often create hubs for recreation and tourism (boating, fishing, lakeside parks), contributing to local economies and job creation. They can provide steady water supply for industrial clusters and agricultural industries, supporting economic stability and planning. Design choices that balance water delivery with recreational access can maximize these benefits.

>Environmental and Social Considerations
Ecosystem and habitat impacts: altered flow regimes, habitat disruption, and potential barriers to fish migration.
Sedimentation and aging: reservoirs fill with sediment over time, reducing storage capacity and altering ecosystem dynamics.
Evaporation and water quality: surface water losses and potential for algal blooms or nutrient buildup if not managed.
Social impacts: displacement of communities, cultural resource considerations, and equity in access to benefits.
Climate change: shifting precipitation patterns can affect reservoir reliability and intensify trade-offs among uses.
Governance: requires transparent decision-making, clear prioritization among uses, and robust monitoring.
>Best Practices for Maximizing Benefits
Implement integrated water resources management (IWRM) to balance drinking, irrigation, industry, energy, and ecological needs.
Engage stakeholders early and maintain ongoing governance with clear performance metrics.
Design for multi-use and flexibility: accommodate water supply, flood control, hydropower, recreation, and ecological needs.
Preserve ecological connectivity: fish passages, bypass channels, and environmental flow requirements.
Manage sediment and water quality: sediment management strategies, watershed protection, and nutrient control.
Monitor and adapt: use data-driven monitoring of storage, releases, ecological indicators, and social outcomes; adjust operations as needed.
>Trade-offs and Mitigation
Trade-offs between water storage, ecological integrity, and downstream flows require careful planning and adaptive management.
Mitigation strategies include maintaining ecological flows, installing fish passage solutions, sediment management, and coordinating with land-use planning to reduce sediment runoff.
Address evaporation losses and climate sensitivity through efficient infrastructure design, cover measures where feasible, and diversifying water sources.
Minimize social disruption through fair compensation, transparent siting decisions, and inclusive stakeholder engagement.
Ensure robust data privacy and governance practices for data used in operations and planning.
