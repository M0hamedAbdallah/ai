## Introduction

By now, you should have a clear understanding of the opportunity and value that the new wave of conversational AI offers us. You have also learned how prompts that we provide can be adjusted or optimized to produce desired response when conversing with an AI model.

In this lab, we\'ll take the so-called standard or \"Naive\" approach. We\'ll query  AI in the simplest way possible, like most people would. The result will be alright, but we\'ll see how a simple adjustment can radically improve the results later in the lab. We\'ll tackle more complex approaches in future labs that produce even better results.

## Learning objectives

After completing this lab, you will be able to:

 - Construct and test naive prompts and persona-based prompts to observe differences in output quality and alignment with intended tasks

 - Analyze the effectiveness of naive versus persona-based prompts by evaluating model responses for coherence, tone, and task relevance

::page{title="The Naive Approach"}

Imagine wanting to get in shape. We could ask our AI for some recommendations. A naive question might be as follows:

```
What is the best way to get fit?
```

Try entering this question in the prompt on the right. Here is the result we got. The output produced at your end might vary, even if you use the same model.


>There isn’t a single “best” method for getting fit that works for everyone. The most effective plan is the one you can stick with consistently, over the long term. A solid approach combines resistance training, cardiovascular work, mobility/recovery, and good nutrition, tailored to your goals (fat loss, muscle gain, endurance, health). Below is a practical, starter-friendly framework you can adapt.

>Key principles

>Consistency over perfection: aim to train regularly, even if some days aren’t perfect.
Resistance training 2–3 days per week: builds/maintains muscle, supports metabolism.
Cardio 2–3 days per week: supports heart health and endurance.
Progressive overload: gradually increase weight, reps, or training density to continue adapting.
Recovery matters: sleep 7–9 hours, include rest days and mobility work.
Nutrition that fits you: adequate protein, sensible calories for your goals, and hydration.
Safety first: warm up, use proper form, and seek guidance if you’re unsure.

>Starter 4-week plan

>Structure (Weeks 1–4):

>Monday: Full-body resistance training
Tuesday: Cardio (steady-state, 20–30 minutes)
Wednesday: Rest or light mobility
Thursday: Full-body resistance training
Friday: Cardio (steady-state, 20–30 minutes) or active recovery
Saturday: Optional light activity or mobility
Sunday: Rest

>Resistance workout outline (choose dumbbells or bodyweight if you’re just starting):

>Goblet squat or bodyweight squat: 3 sets of 8–12 reps
Dumbbell bench press or push-ups: 3x8–12
Dumbbell row or inverted row: 3x8–12
Dumbbell Romanian deadlift or hip hinge: 3x8–12
Overhead press (dumbbells or barbell): 3x8–12
Plank: 3 rounds of 30–45 seconds

>Progression rules:

>Each week, try to add 1–2 reps per set or increase weight slightly if the movement feels comfortable.
Rest 60–90 seconds between sets.
Prioritize technique; start lighter to learn form before adding load.

>Nutrition basics

>Protein: aim for about 1.6–2.2 g per kilogram of body weight per day to support muscle maintenance and growth.
Calories: create a sustainable energy balance.
For fat loss: slight deficit (e.g., 300–500 kcal/day).
For muscle gain: slight surplus (e.g., 100–300 kcal/day) when feasible.
Carbs and fats: balance to fit your preferences and training needs; both are important for performance.
Hydration: target roughly 2–3 liters per day, adjusting for size and activity level.
Meal timing: a protein-rich meal around workouts can help recovery, but total daily intake matters more than exact timing.
Tracking: use a simple system (baseline calories and protein, adjust every 2–4 weeks).

>Getting started and safety

>Check with a healthcare provider if you have medical conditions, are pregnant, or haven’t exercised before.
Start with a proper warm-up: 5–10 minutes of light cardio plus dynamic stretches.
Learn proper form: consider a few sessions with a trainer or high-quality instructional videos before lifting heavy.
Listen to your body: rest if you feel pain, dizziness, or severe fatigue; adjust intensity as needed.
Set SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) and track progress (notes, photos, weights, reps).
Build habit loops: schedule workouts, prepare equipment, and keep travel-friendly workout options.

```
Acting as a fitness expert, tell me the best way to get fit.
```

The advice is still a little generic, but it\'s a vast improvement over the naive approach.

You\'ll also notice that we didn\'t use the PROMPT INSTRUCTIONS field. We could have easily embedded the persona in that field by saying something like, \"You\'ll act as a fitness expert\".

We can also augment the basic persona pattern with specific instructions about the format or specifics of the answers in the PROMPT INSTRUCTIONS field. For example, we could instruct the bot to use bullet points for its replies or format responses in tables whenever possible.

::page{title="Taking it a step further"}

Let\'s take it a step further and see how using a persona affects creating a workout plan.

In the PROMPT INSTRUCTIONS field, let\'s add the following:

```
You will act as a fitness expert who is current with the latest research data and provide very detailed step-by-step instructions in reply to my queries.
```

You\'ll see that we structured this prompt in three parts:

1. We told the LLM the persona it should act as.
2. We specified a qualifier about being research-based.
3. We told AI the response format we wanted.

Now we can enter the actual query in our prompt.

```
Create a gym workout program to lose weight and build strength for an out-of-shape beginner.
```

You can try it yourself, but as you will notice in the screenshot below, we got quite detailed instructions and a decent training plan!

![gpt_9.png](https://cf-courses-data.s3.us.cloud-object-storage.appdomain.cloud/tX0gOgDDa9J3zHbpPWM8gQ/gpt-9.png)


>Welcome! Below is a practical, evidence-informed 8-week gym program designed for an out-of-shape beginner who wants to lose weight and build strength. It combines full-body resistance training with cardio, emphasizes technique and gradual progression, and includes clear cues, regression options, and progression paths. Always check with a healthcare provider before starting any new exercise program if you have medical conditions.

>**Program goals and design principles**

> * Primary goals: promote weight loss through a modest daily energy deficit, build lean muscle, and improve movement quality.
> * Training frequency: resistance training 3 days/week (full body) + 2–3 cardio sessions per week.
> * Progression approach: progressive overload via small weight increases and/or reps, with emphasis on perfecting form first.
> * Reps/sets for beginners: start with 2–3 sets of 8–12 reps for most exercises; adjust tempo and rest to sustain good form.
> * Tempo cue: controlled tempo with a deliberate 2-second eccentric (lowering), 0-second pause, and controlled concentric (lifting) to max out quality and time under tension.
> * Recovery: ensure at least 48 hours between heavy resistance sessions for the same muscle groups; include light activity on rest days as desired (e.g., walking).
> * Safety: prioritize technique over load, stop if pain (not normal muscle fatigue) occurs, and modify exercises as needed.

>**Weekly layout (8-week cycle)**

> * Training days (3x/week): Monday, Wednesday, Friday – Full-body resistance workouts.
> * Cardio days (2–3x/week): Tuesday and Saturday are recommended for cardio; optional light cardio on Sunday or after workouts if you feel up to it.
> * Rest days: Thursday and Sunday are built-in rest days or very light activity days based on how you feel.
> * Progression cadence: each 2 weeks, aim to increase either weight by a small amount (e.g., 2.5–5 lbs for dumbbells; 5–10 lbs for barbell or equivalent machines) or one additional rep per set (within the 8–12 rep range) while maintaining good form.

>**Resistance training template (per session)**

> * Warm-up: 5–10 minutes of light cardio (e.g., brisk walk) + dynamic mobility (hip circles, leg swings, arm circles).
> * Core activation: 1–2 minutes of abdominal bracing before starting lifts.
*Main lifts (full-body):
>1. Goblet Squat or Bodyweight Squat – 2–3 sets x 8–12 reps
>2. Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift (or Hip Hinge) – 2–3 sets x 8–12 reps
>3.  Dumbbell Bench Press or Push-Up (incline if needed) – 2–3 sets x 8–12 reps
>4. One-Arm Dumbbell Row or Seated Row – 2–3 sets x 8–12 reps per side
>5. Dumbbell Shoulder Press – 2–3 sets x 8–12 reps
>6. Optional: Lat Pulldown or Assisted Pull-Up – 2–3 sets x 8–12 reps

> * Core & grip: 1–2 core exercises (e.g., Plank 20–40s, Dead Bug 8–12 reps per side) and a farmer’s carry variation if equipment allows – 2–3 sets, 20–40 seconds or 20–40 meters
> * Cool-down: 5–10 minutes of light stretching and deep breathing
*Rest: 60–90 seconds between sets (adjust as needed for form and fatigue)

>**Example exercise library (technique cues and regressions)**

> * Goblet Squat: Stand with feet shoulder-width, hold a dumbbell/kettlebell at chest. Sit back and down, keeping chest up and knees tracking over toes. Stand up by driving through heels. Regression: sit-to-stand from a chair; progression: goblet held higher or add tempo.
> * Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift: Hinge at hips with slight knee bend, neutral spine, weight close to thighs, descend until you feel a stretch in hamstrings, then return to start. Regression: reduced range or lighter weight; progression: single-leg variant.
> * Dumbbell Bench Press / Push-Up: Keep elbows at ~45 degrees, press through chest, avoid flaring elbows. Regression: incline push-ups or floor press; progression: incline to flat bench or add tempo.
> * One-Arm Dumbbell Row / Seated Row: Brace core, hinge at hips slightly, pull elbow toward ribcage, squeeze shoulder blade. Regression: supported or bent-over with lighter weight; progression: increase weight or tempo control.
> * Lat Pulldown / Assisted Pull-Up: Reach long, pull down to upper chest, control on the way up. Regression: resistance band row or machine row; progression: heavier lat pulldown or full pull-up with assistance reduced.
> * Dumbbell Shoulder Press: Press overhead with a stable core, avoid excessive low back arch. Regression: seated or alternating press; progression: heavier dumbbells or tempo.
> * Farmer’s Carry / Suitcase Carry: Hold dumbbells at sides, brace core, walk straight. Regression: lighter weights or shorter distance; progression: heavier weights or longer carry.
> * Core work: Plank (hold 20–40s with good form), Dead Bug (hands/legs moving opposite and controlled). Regression: knee-supported plank; progression: longer holds or added time under tension.

>**8-week progression plan (simple roadmap)**

> * Weeks 1–2: Build technique and confidence. 2 sets per exercise, 12–15 reps, 60–90 seconds rest. Focus on form and full range of motion.
> * Weeks 3–4: Move to 3 sets per exercise, 10–12 reps, 60–90 seconds rest. Begin modest weekly weight increases if technique is solid.
> * Weeks 5–6: Continue 3 sets, 8–12 reps with a clearer emphasis on progressive overload (add 2.5–5 lb dumbbell increases or equivalent). Consider adding tempo (2–0–2–0) to the eccentric phase.
> * Weeks 7–8: Maintain 3 sets of 6–10 reps with heavier weights if form remains solid. You may add one extra cardio session or extend one cardio session by 5–10 minutes if energy allows.
> * General: If you hit 12 reps with perfect form, consider a small weight increase in the next set. If form degrades, drop weight and focus on technique.

>**Cardio plan and progression**

> * Options: (A) 2–3 sessions/wk of moderate-intensity cardio (e.g., brisk walking, cycling, elliptical) for 20–30 minutes; (B) 1–2 sessions/wk of interval training (e.g., 1–2 minutes hard, 1–2 minutes easy, repeat 4–6 times).
> * Intensity: Aim for a Level 5–7/10 feeling breathy but able to maintain conversation (moderate) during steady-state cardio; for intervals, push to RPE 8–9 during hard bouts.
> * Progression: Increase duration by 5 minutes every 1–2 weeks or add one extra hard interval as tolerated.
> * Integration: Schedule cardio on non-lifting days or after resistance workouts if energy allows.

>**Nutrition and recovery considerations**

> * Calorie balance: A modest deficit (e.g., 250–500 kcal/day) generally supports weight loss without excessive fatigue; focus on protein intake (about 1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight per day) to preserve lean mass.
> * Hydration: 2–3 liters/day (adjust for body size and climate).
> * Sleep: 7–9 hours per night if possible to aid recovery and appetite regulation.
> * Recovery strategies: light activity on rest days (walks, stretching), mobility work, and foam rolling as needed.

>**Tracking progress and practical tips**

> * Metrics: weight (weekly), waist/hip measurements (monthly), strength benchmarks (e.g., max reps with given weight within form), and session RPE tracking.
> * Start easy, finish strong: prioritize form over loading; write down reps and weight so you can progress weekly.
> * Common pitfalls: skipping warm-ups, overloading too soon, ignoring pain signals; address with regression or rest when needed.
> * Accessibility: If gym access is limited, substitute moves with bodyweight equivalents or resistance bands.

>**Getting started checklist**

> * Clear any medical concerns with your doctor.
*Gather equipment: dumbbells or adjustable weights, a resistance band, a bench or sturdy elevated surface, and a cardio option (t loop, treadmill, bike).
*Set goals and a simple calendar (e.g., 3 resistance days + 2 cardio days per week).
> * Learn the 6 core movements and track your first 2 weeks of workouts before adjusting intensity.



Before starting any new exercise program, especially if you have any health concerns, it\'s advisable to consult with a healthcare provider or a fitness professional. Enjoy your journey to fitness!

The only fundamental limitation here is that the program we received is not custom made for us. It\'s for a generic beginner who is out of shape. Is it a man or a woman? Is it a young person or an 82 year old? Can they do any exercise or are there mobility issues? All of that gets ignored.


## When the persona is someone famous

We can also instruct AI to channel a famous person to get a more specific response style. In the following example, we ask AI to list 10 articles to promote our hypothetical dog training book. We then ask it to act as marketing expert Seth Godin (known for his out-of-the-box marketing mind), and we get significantly more exciting suggestions.

Here is the naive prompt that we use:

```
Give me a list of 10 article titles to promote my new book about dog training.
```

And the prompt yields the following result:

>Here are 10 article titles to promote your new dog training book:

> 1. The Ultimate Dog Training Playbook: Techniques That Create Lasting Obedience
> 2. From Puppy to Pro: A Practical Roadmap for Confident, Well-Behaved Dogs
> 3. Calm, Connected, and In Control: Build a Strong Bond Through Training
> 4. Real-World Training Essentials: Recall, Leash Manners, and House Training Made Simple
> 5. Solving Barking and Impulse Issues: Gentle, Effective Behavior Techniques
> 6. Focus, Fun, and Fitness: Training Moves That Challenge Your Dog's Mind and Body
> 7. Reactive to Reliable: Training Strategies for Dogs with Triggers and Fears
> 8. Positive Reinforcement Masterclass: The Science Behind Lasting Change
> 9. Training in Small Spaces: Effective Techniques for Apartment Living
> 10. The Owner-Dog Duo: Turn Training Sessions into Quality Time

>**Usage tips**

>Pair each title with a concise subtitle and a 2-3 sentence synopsis. Use these titles as blog post hooks, email newsletter subjects, or social media captions. Consider creating a mix of how-to, psychology, and real-world problem-solving angles to appeal to both new dog owners and experienced handlers. Include a clear call-to-action directing readers to the book landing page or purchase link.


This too is not bad but, again, quite generic and bland.

Here is the prompt using the style of the aforementioned famous marketer:

```
Acting as marketing expert Seth Godin, give me a list of 10 article titles to promote my new book about dog training.
```

This is the result received:

>Seth Godin-inspired article ideas to promote your dog training book

> 1. The Purple-Cow Approach to Dog Training: Make Your Book Unmissable

> * Subtitle: Stand out in a crowded market by turning routine dog training into a remarkable movement.
> * Synopsis: Apply Seth Godin's Purple Cow principles to your book: create a truly remarkable training method, cultivate a distinct voice, and invite readers to join a community. This piece outlines how to differentiate your book, craft an audacious promise, and spark word-of-mouth.

> 2. Tribes, Not Tricks: Build a Community Around Your Dog-Training Book

> * Subtitle: Grow a loyal following by serving a dedicated audience of dog lovers and handlers.
> * Synopsis: Define your reader tribe, give them a shared language, and empower them to become ambassadors. Explore strategies for creating ongoing engagement and letting the tribe do the marketing for you.

> 3. The Dip of Great Dog Training: Keep Readers Through the Last Chapter

> * Subtitle: Anticipate reader fatigue and design content that sustains momentum.
> * Synopsis: Borrowing from The Dip, outline how to guide readers through skill-building sections with previews, real-world stories, and checkpoints that keep momentum from stalling.

> 4. Permission Marketing for Pups: Earn Attention Without Clickbait

> * Subtitle: Value-first engagement that respects readers' time.
> * Synopsis: Show how ethical list-building, meaningful freebies, and solid onboarding can build trust. Frame marketing as an invitation, not a shout, so readers feel compelled to follow along.

> 5. Ship It: Releasing Your Dog Training Book Before It's Perfect

> * Subtitle: The disciplined art of iterative launches and real-world feedback.
> * Synopsis: Argue for an MVP approach, beta readers, and ongoing updates. Demonstrate how early feedback shapes chapters, exercises, and the ultimate impact on readers.

> 6. Linchpin Trainers: Become Indispensable to Dogs and Their Humans

> * Subtitle: Position yourself as an essential resource in the dog-training journey.
> * Synopsis: Explain how to offer unique value that goes beyond the book—templates, checklists, and access to a community—that makes readers rely on your expertise.

> 7. The 1% Advantage: Tiny, Consistent Training Habits That Build Loyal Readers

> * Subtitle: Small, reliable actions compound into big outcomes for dogs and owners.
> * Synopsis: Promote micro-habits and bite-sized actions that readers can implement daily. Show how consistent, tiny wins create trust and long-term engagement with the book and brand.

> 8. Story-Driven Training: How Narrative Sells Better Than Rules

> * Subtitle: Use compelling dog-owner stories to teach principles.
> * Synopsis: Argue for structuring chapters around story arcs, case studies, and emotional payoff. Explain how narrative makes technique memorable and shareable.

> 9. Ask Better Questions: Transforming Dog Training From Commands to Conversations

> * Subtitle: Engage readers by reframing training into dialogue with their dog.
> * Synopsis: Encourage readers to explore questions that lead to deeper understanding, not just obedience. Provide prompts and frameworks that readers can apply with their dogs.

> 10. Ethical Marketing for the Dog Book: Confidence, Clarity, and Compassion

> * Subtitle: Market with integrity while delivering real value.
> * Synopsis: Emphasize honest messaging, transparent claims, and value-driven launches. Show how integrity builds a durable audience who will advocate for your book.


> 
These titles focus on storytelling, building community, and shifting paradigms, which are key elements in Godin\'s marketing philosophy. They encourage readers to think differently about dog training and to engage with your book as a transformative resource.


This is much more compelling, and all it took was a few extra words to our prompt. That\'s the power of Prompt Engineering!

## Exercises

1. Try a few queries of your own. Start with a generic, naive prompt and then improve it by adding a persona.

```
You'll act as a liberal political expert and as a conservative political expert and provide two answers for each question I ask.
```

```
Which policies should be implemented to address inflation?
```
